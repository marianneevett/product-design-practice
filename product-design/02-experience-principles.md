# Experience Principles

Experience principles are a small set of rules used to make consistent product decisions when the answer isn't obvious.

They translate what we've learned about people, the business, and the system into guidance that can be applied across features, flows, and implementation decisions.

They are not aspirations or brand values.

A useful principle should help us choose between two reasonable options.

---

## Start with the tension

Before writing a principle, identify the recurring tension behind it.

Examples:

* Flexibility vs. simplicity
* Speed vs. confidence
* Business requirements vs. customer control
* Automation vs. transparency
* Consistency vs. contextual relevance
* Power vs. cognitive load

The principle should tell us how we intend to navigate that tension.

# Example principles

These are examples rather than universal rules. Principles should emerge from the specific product and problem.

## Make the next step clear

People shouldn't need to understand the entire system before they can move forward.

**This means we**

* Establish a clear primary action
* Reveal information when it becomes relevant
* Explain consequences before consequential decisions
* Preserve context between steps

**We avoid**

* Presenting every possible action with equal emphasis
* Requiring users to understand internal system logic
* Introducing decisions before they're necessary

---

## Preserve intent

When someone has already expressed what they want, the product should carry that intent forward whenever possible.

**This means we**

* Retain meaningful selections and preferences
* Avoid asking for the same information repeatedly
* Make changes and consequences visible
* Allow people to recover when circumstances change

**We avoid**

* Resetting progress without reason
* Translating system constraints into unnecessary customer work
* Treating every step as an isolated interaction

---

## Make complexity useful

Complex systems don't always need simple functionality. They need complexity presented at the right moment and at the right level.

**This means we**

* Simplify the experience without hiding important consequences
* Separate primary information from supporting detail
* Introduce advanced controls when they become useful
* Design for both common paths and meaningful exceptions

**We avoid**

* Removing necessary capability in the name of simplicity
* Exposing internal complexity simply because it exists
* Designing every edge case as though it were the primary experience

---

## Creating the final set

Start with observations rather than slogans.

Ask:

1. What problems keep appearing across the experience?
2. Where are teams repeatedly making inconsistent decisions?
3. What do we know about how people approach these situations?
4. What tensions won't disappear even after we redesign the product?
5. What rule would help us make better decisions repeatedly?

Aim for **3–5 principles**.

If there are twelve, they probably aren't principles yet.

---

## Test the principles

For each principle, ask:

**Can it change a decision?**

If two reasonable solutions exist, the principle should help us choose.

**Can it be challenged?**

A principle should express a point of view rather than something everyone automatically agrees with.

**Can it survive beyond one feature?**

It should apply across enough of the experience to be useful repeatedly.

**Can we see it in the product?**

Someone should eventually be able to point to an interaction, workflow, or product decision and explain how the principle influenced it.

---

## Keep them alive

Principles aren't finished because they've been documented.

Revisit them when:

* New evidence challenges an assumption
* The product or business model changes
* Teams repeatedly encounter a new tension
* A principle stops helping people make decisions

The goal is not to protect the wording.

**The goal is to protect the reasoning behind it.**
